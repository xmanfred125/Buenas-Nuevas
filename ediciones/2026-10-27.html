Three Arrested After Funeral Massacre at a Church in JordanTres detenidos tras la masacre en un funeral en una iglesia de Jordania

Jordanian security forces arrested three men on October 26 over the October 23 shooting at a funeral in Karak that killed nine mourners and injured dozens, an attack the government has called terrorism.Las fuerzas de seguridad jordanas detuvieron el 26 de octubre a tres hombres por el tiroteo del 23 de octubre en un funeral en Karak, que mató a nueve personas e hirió a decenas, un ataque que el gobierno calificó de terrorismo.

Abba Post · October 26, 2026Abba Post · 26 de octubre de 2026

Three men were arrested on Monday, October 26, in connection with a mass shooting that turned a funeral into a scene of horror in Karak, Jordan. On Friday, October 23, gunmen armed with AK-47 rifles opened fire on 150 to 200 mourners gathered for a funeral at St. George Church, killing nine people and injuring 40, according to Jordanian officials.

Interior Minister Mazen Al-Faraya described the attack as terrorism. Speaking on public television, he said the security services had fulfilled their duties and captured those who carried out the act, who resisted fiercely before being subdued and taken into custody with severe injuries. The three suspects have not been identified as members of a terrorist group.

Jordan declared three days of national mourning. The attack was the first on a church or Christian building in the country in at least two years: the 2026 World Watch List from Open Doors counted no such attacks in Jordan in its October 2024 to September 2025 reporting period, and only one in the previous year.

International Christian Concern called the assault a heinous attack on the universal right to worship freely, noting with particular distress that it targeted people gathered in a church for mourning, remembrance and solidarity. Churches and human rights groups across the region joined in condemning the violence and praying for the bereaved.

For Jordan's Christian community, long known for living in relative peace alongside its Muslim neighbours, the shooting is a wound that will take time to heal. The arrests bring a measure of accountability, but the deeper question remains: how a place of worship, at the most vulnerable human moment, became a target at all.

El ministro del Interior, Mazen Al-Faraya, describió el ataque como terrorismo. En la televisión pública dijo que los servicios de seguridad cumplieron su deber y capturaron a quienes perpetraron el acto, que opusieron una feroz resistencia antes de ser reducidos y detenidos con heridas graves. Los tres sospechosos no han sido identificados como miembros de un grupo terrorista.

International Christian Concern calificó el asalto como un ataque atroz contra el derecho universal a adorar libremente, señalando con particular consternación que se dirigió contra personas reunidas en una iglesia para el duelo, el recuerdo y la solidaridad. Abba Post · October 27, 2026Abba Post · 27 de octubre de 2026

At least 60 people were killed in two separate attacks in Ethiopia's conflict-hit Oromiya region in the past week, the Ethiopian Orthodox Church said on Monday, October 26. Church authorities said all of the dead were Christians, though they did not give details of how they died or who was behind the attacks.

According to the Holy Synod of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, 14 believers were killed in Merti Woreda on October 20 and 46 in Sherka Woreda on October 24. More than 20 homes were burned and more than 20 people were hospitalised with severe injuries.

Blame for the killings is fiercely contested. The Oromiya regional government said a rebel alliance launched the attacks to try to spark religious conflict, without providing evidence. A source linked to the rebel alliance said instead that the regional government had carried out the killings, citing conversations with two local residents.

The Oromo Liberation Army, which the regional government has blamed for previous attacks on Orthodox Christians, did not immediately respond to requests for comment. The group is one of seven armed groups that announced last month that they had formed an alliance aimed at overthrowing Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government.

Behind the competing claims are grieving families and burned villages, and a Christian community caught in a conflict it did not choose. For the wider Church, the statement from the Holy Synod is a call to pray for the bereaved, for the injured, and for a peace in Oromiya that protects the innocent whatever flag their attackers fly.

Al menos 60 personas fueron asesinadas en dos ataques distintos en la región de Oromiya, en Etiopía, afectada por el conflicto, durante la última semana, informó la Iglesia Ortodoxa Etíope el lunes 26 de octubre. Las autoridades eclesiales dijeron que todos los muertos eran cristianos, aunque no dieron detalles sobre cómo murieron ni quién estuvo detrás de los ataques.

Según el Santo Sínodo de la Iglesia Ortodoxa Tewahedo de Etiopía, 14 creyentes fueron asesinados en Merti Woreda el 20 de octubre y 46 en Sherka Woreda el 24 de octubre. Más de 20 casas fueron quemadas y más de 20 personas fueron hospitalizadas con heridas graves.

La responsabilidad de los asesinatos se disputa con ferocidad. El gobierno regional de Oromiya dijo que una alianza rebelde lanzó los ataques para intentar provocar un conflicto religioso, sin aportar pruebas. Una fuente vinculada a la alianza rebelde dijo, en cambio, que el gobierno regional había perpetrado los asesinatos, citando conversaciones con dos residentes locales.

El Ejército de Liberación Oromo, al que el gobierno regional ha culpado de ataques anteriores contra cristianos ortodoxos, no respondió de inmediato a las solicitudes de comentarios. El grupo es uno de los siete grupos armados que anunciaron el mes pasado que habían formado una alianza con el objetivo de derrocar al gobierno del primer ministro Abiy Ahmed.

Detrás de las acusaciones enfrentadas hay familias en duelo y aldeas quemadas, y una comunidad cristiana atrapada en un conflicto que no eligió. Para la Iglesia en general, la declaración del Santo Sínodo es un llamado a orar por los afligidos, por los heridos y por una paz en Oromiya que proteja a los inocentes, sea cual sea la bandera de sus atacantes.

Former pastor CJ Johnson has launched Patmos Church, a new congregation born in the wake of his dismissal from Northview Church. The announcement, highlighted among this week's leading Christian headlines, marks a fresh chapter for a pastor whose departure from his former church drew wide attention in evangelical circles.

The name itself carries a message. Patmos is the island where the apostle John, exiled for the word of God, received the Revelation. For a new church, it is a name that speaks of faithfulness in a hard place, of vision given not in comfort but in exile, and of a God who meets his people where they are sent, not only where they planned to be.

New churches are often born out of disruption. Sometimes they arise from growth, sometimes from division, and sometimes, as here, from a painful ending that becomes a beginning. What will determine Patmos Church's future is not the circumstances of its founding but the ordinary work ahead: preaching, prayer, pastoral care and the slow building of a community that holds together week after week.

For Johnson and those gathering with him, the first Sundays will be a test of a simple question: can a congregation formed in controversy become a congregation formed by the gospel? Church plants across America face that question in quieter ways every year, and the answer is rarely given in a launch announcement. It is given over months of sermons, small groups, baptisms and funerals.

Whatever the wider debate about his dismissal, the launch is a reminder of a pattern as old as the Church itself: God often builds something new out of what looked like an ending. Patmos Church now begins the long work of proving, Sunday by Sunday, that its name is more than a story, it is a calling.

Abba Post · October 26, 2026Abba Post · 26 de octubre de 2026

Quebec has long been Exhibit A in the story of secularisation in North America. In a single generation, a province once defined by its churches emptied them, and faith became a private matter, rarely discussed and seldom practised. That is what makes the latest signal so striking: young Quebecers are turning toward evangelicalism.

The trend, noted in this week's evangelical roundup, fits a pattern observers have begun to trace among young adults elsewhere: a generation raised without religion, or with only its cultural shell, is discovering that secular answers to life's biggest questions do not satisfy. They are looking for community, for moral clarity, and for a faith that asks something of them rather than merely affirming them.

Evangelical churches, with their emphasis on personal conversion, Bible teaching and gathered worship, offer a contrast to both the inherited religion many young Quebecers left behind and the expressive individualism that replaced it. For a young adult in Montreal or Quebec City, walking into an evangelical congregation is often not a return to something familiar, but a discovery of something entirely new.

The movement is still young, and its scale should not be exaggerated. Quebec remains a deeply secular province, and most young Quebecers are not in any church on a Sunday morning. But revivals rarely begin with majorities. They begin with a few, in a few rooms, asking whether the gospel might be true after all.

If the trend holds, its implications reach beyond Quebec. A province that secularised faster than almost anywhere in the West could become a case study in the opposite direction: not of faith's inevitable decline, but of its surprising persistence among the very generation expected to leave it behind.

Quebec ha sido durante mucho tiempo el ejemplo principal de la historia de la secularización en América del Norte. En una sola generación, una provincia definida antes por sus iglesias las vació, y la fe se convirtió en un asunto privado, rara vez discutido y pocas veces practicado. Eso es lo que hace tan llamativa la señal más reciente: los jóvenes quebequenses se están volviendo hacia el evangelicalismo.

La tendencia, señalada en el resumen evangélico de la semana, encaja con un patrón que los observadores han comenzado a trazar entre los jóvenes adultos en otros lugares: una generación criada sin religión, o solo con su cáscara cultural, está descubriendo que las respuestas seculares a las grandes preguntas de la vida no satisfacen. Buscan comunidad, claridad moral y una fe que les exija algo, en lugar de limitarse a afirmarles.

Las iglesias evangélicas, con su énfasis en la conversión personal, la enseñanza bíblica y el culto congregacional, ofrecen un contraste tanto con la religión heredada que muchos jóvenes quebequenses dejaron atrás como con el individualismo expresivo que la reemplazó. Para un joven adulto en Montreal o en la ciudad de Quebec, entrar en una congregación evangélica a menudo no es un regreso a algo familiar, sino el descubrimiento de algo completamente nuevo.

El movimiento aún es joven y no hay que exagerar su magnitud. Quebec sigue siendo una provincia profundamente secular, y la mayoría de los jóvenes quebequenses no están en ninguna iglesia un domingo por la mañana. Pero los avivamientos rara vez comienzan con mayorías. Comienzan con unos pocos, en unas pocas habitaciones, preguntándose si el evangelio podría ser verdad después de todo.

Si la tendencia se mantiene, sus implicaciones van más allá de Quebec. Una provincia que se secularizó más rápido que casi cualquier lugar de Occidente podría convertirse en un caso de estudio en la dirección opuesta: no del declive inevitable de la fe, sino de su sorprendente persistencia entre la misma generación de la que se esperaba que la abandonara.

Christianity Today, the flagship magazine of American evangelicalism founded in 1956, is in turmoil. A New York Times report by Ruth Graham this week described serious layoffs, accusations of administrative interference with editorial decisions, and a recent shift in the publication's direction away from its historic Wheaton identity and toward a TikTok-era audience.

The report has stirred a wide conversation among evangelicals, including former staff. Former Christianity Today journalist Daniel Silliman, former editor Katelyn Beaty and writer Bonnie Kristian have all weighed in publicly, adding context and, in some cases, sharp criticism of the magazine's handling of its own newsroom.

Into that debate stepped O. Alan Noble, associate professor of English at Oklahoma Baptist University, with an essay arguing that the magazine's freefall is bad news. Christians need more institutions like Christianity Today, he wrote, not fewer: serious, independent places where evangelical faith is reported, examined and argued about with honesty rather than reduced to slogans.

The stakes, in Noble's telling, go beyond one magazine's fortunes. Evangelicalism without trustworthy institutions of its own is left to the mercy of algorithms, outrage and celebrity, where the loudest voice wins and careful reporting loses. A publication that can tell the Church hard truths about itself is not a luxury; it is part of how a movement stays honest.

Whether Christianity Today recovers its footing remains an open question, and its readers will be watching closely. But the debate itself is a sign of health: a movement willing to argue, in public, about the institutions that carry its voice is a movement that still believes that voice matters.

The policy, like similar rules adopted in districts across the country, had required school staff to withhold information from parents when a student chose a different name, pronouns or gender identity at school. Supporters of such policies argued they protected students who might face difficult reactions at home. Critics, including many Christian parents, argued that schools have no right to come between parents and their children.

For Christian families, the issue touches a conviction that runs deeper than politics: that parents, not institutions, bear the God-given responsibility for raising their children. When a school keeps secrets from parents, these families argue, it does not merely adopt a policy, it claims an authority that belongs to the home.

The New Jersey reversal is part of a wider national shift. Across the United States, courts, legislatures and school boards have been reconsidering policies that separate parents from decisions about their children's wellbeing, and parental-rights advocates have welcomed each reversal as a step back toward transparency.

The debate is far from over, and other districts still maintain similar policies. But for parents who have prayed for schools that tell the truth to families, the New Jersey decision is a reminder that policies can change, and that speaking up, persistently and peacefully, still makes a difference.

Su listado oficial de vinilos firmados confirma una donación del 100% de las ganancias netas, no del precio total de compra: la discográfica aún toma su porcentaje, pero el dinero que él y su equipo recibirían de otro modo es la porción que están donando.

La idea tomó forma mientras Queen oraba sobre a dónde debía ir el dinero. Dijo que ya llevaba unos cuatro días firmando CD cuando describió el proyecto a Miller. Entonces un video de Instagram interrumpió el desplazamiento: Miller hablando de la iglesia perseguida y entrevistando a un pastor nigeriano, y la necesidad se volvió personal.

Queen quería ayudar, pero no sabía por dónde empezar. Finalmente envió un mensaje directo a Miller, y Miller lo llamó ese mismo día para conversar sobre las posibilidades. Lo que siguió fue una alianza entre un artista, un podcast y un ministerio de ayuda, con las ganancias de la música convertidas en ayuda para creyentes que pagan por su fe con mucho más que dinero.

Para los fans, cada copia firmada ahora tiene un segundo significado. No es solo un álbum: es un regalo transmitido: música comprada en Estados Unidos convertida en comida, refugio, Biblias o ayuda legal para una familia cristiana al otro lado del mundo. Eso es generosidad radical, y Queen lo sabe. Simplemente piensa que el evangelio no pide nada menos.

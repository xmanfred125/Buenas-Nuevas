Two Baptist Seminaries Cut Ties with Kanakuk Institute Over Abuse-Linked CampDos seminarios bautistas cortan lazos con Kanakuk tras revelarse sus vínculos con el campamento

Southern Baptist Theological Seminary ended its credit-transfer agreement with the Kanakuk Institute "effective immediately," and Midwestern Seminary also broke its relationship, after reporting showed the institute and the abuse-accused camp share leadership, staff, and property.Southern Baptist Theological Seminary terminó «con efecto inmediato» su acuerdo de transferencia de créditos con el Instituto Kanakuk, y Midwestern Seminary también rompió su relación, tras revelarse que el instituto y el campamento acusado de abusos comparten directivos, personal y propiedades.

November 20, 202620 de noviembre de 2026

Southern Baptist Theological Seminary (SBTS) has ended its credit-transfer agreement with the Kanakuk Institute "effective immediately," president R. Albert Mohler Jr. wrote to journalist Nancy French on November 20. The eight-month program for college graduates is linked to Kanakuk Kamps in Missouri, which faces accusations of child sexual abuse. Current students will not lose their credits, but no new ones will be granted.

French documented that the institute and the camp share executives, staff, offices, and property. The seminary had previously believed they were separate entities. Midwestern Baptist Theological Seminary also ended its relationship with the institute, and Belhaven University revoked the honorary doctorate it awarded in 2006 to Kanakuk CEO Joe White.

The institute is not accredited; SBTS had accepted up to 18 hours of credit toward a degree, and the program costs about $26,000. Kanakuk reports 11,000 campers in 2026, a sign of how large — and how trusted — the camp's brand remains among Christian families.

The decisions mark a turning point in a story that has haunted Southern Baptist life for years. For a long time, institutions treated Kanakuk as a separate, reputable ministry that happened to share a name with a camp facing terrible allegations. French's reporting made that separation impossible to maintain.

The lesson reaches beyond one camp. When a ministry's reputation rests on trust, institutions that lend it their name share responsibility for what happens under it. The seminaries' break is late, but it is right — and for the survivors whose voices made it unavoidable, it is a small measure of the justice they have waited years to see.

Rockstar Games launched Grand Theft Auto VI on November 19 for PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X/S, with a pre-download of 209 GB — one of the biggest entertainment releases in history. Within a day, British commentator Peter Hitchens had published his verdict in the Daily Mail: the game is "electronic poison" that "undermines 2,000 years of Christian civilization."

Hitchens wrote that he searched Vice City, the fictional Miami-inspired setting, for a church where his character could confess — and found no way to do so. He argues that the game rewards theft and extortion, with no rules and no moral consequences. Later reports identified a church, Sunside Church, in Vice City, though it cannot be entered; Hitchens found no confession interaction.

The debate spread quickly through Christian media. Pastor Landon Chase argued in Premier Christianity that no Christian man can play the game without sinning. Rapzilla collected divided voices among rappers, pastors, and Christian creators — some calling for a boycott, others saying the game is a work of fiction to be engaged with discernment, not fear.

The argument is not new. Every generation of Christians has faced a cultural product that seemed to embody the age's moral confusion, from rock music to violent films. What makes GTA VI different is its scale: tens of millions of players will spend hundreds of hours inside a world where crime pays and nothing is sacred.

Hitchens' question deserves a serious answer, not a shrug. If a game trains the imagination to enjoy what God hates, the cost is not measured in dollars or hours, but in the slow reshaping of a conscience. Whether the answer is abstinence or careful engagement, Christians should at least ask the question before they press start.

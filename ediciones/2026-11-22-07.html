Displaced Evangelicals in Oaxaca Denounce Burned Church and Three Missing WomenEvangélicos desplazados en Oaxaca denuncian un templo quemado y tres mujeres desaparecidas

Evangelical families from Cerro Cajón, Oaxaca, say their church was burned, their homes destroyed and three women have disappeared after months of harassment by neighbors.Familias evangélicas de Cerro Cajón, Oaxaca, denuncian que su templo fue quemado, sus hogares destruidos y tres mujeres han desaparecido tras meses de hostigamiento vecinal.

November 19, 202619 de noviembre de 2026

Evangelical families from Cerro Cajón, in the municipality of San Juan Lalana, Oaxaca, have denounced the disappearance of three women, the theft of livestock, the destruction of their homes and their church, and their forced displacement. The complaint was filed this week with the regional prosecutor's office in the Cuenca del Papaloapan, and the Fellowship of Pastors of Oaxaca denounced on Thursday the poor response of the state government.

According to regional pastor Yeraldo Hernández García, attackers burned the church, seized land and livestock, and destroyed the homes of 13 Christians. The displaced families — about 150 families, roughly 700 people — say their village is besieged and without access. Two pastors, Moisés Sarmiento Osuna and Pablo Gómez Sosa, were stripped naked, jailed and threatened with death when they went to support the families.

The conflict began in November 2025 and worsened two weeks ago, according to the complainants. Oaxaca has long been one of Mexico's most difficult states for religious minorities: a recent report counted outbreaks of intolerance in 417 of the state's 570 municipalities, often driven by local customs that punish those who refuse to take part in community religious festivals.

For the families of Cerro Cajón, the cost of their faith has been everything they owned. They left behind houses, land and animals, and now they are asking the authorities for what should be the most basic of guarantees: the right to live in their own village without being driven out for what they believe.

Their pastors say they will keep pressing the case — and keep praying. In a state where impunity has long protected aggressors, the courage of these families is itself a testimony: they lost their homes, but they have not lost their faith.

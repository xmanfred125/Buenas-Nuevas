In Spain, a First Church Joins the Confessional Anglican Movement Breaking with CanterburyEn España, una primera iglesia se suma al movimiento anglicano confesional que rompe con Canterbury

A newly formed evangelical Anglican community in Valencia has formally joined the Anglican Convocation Europe, becoming the first congregation in Spain to align publicly with the global confessional movement that broke with the Church of England over the authority of Scripture.Una comunidad anglicana evangélica recién formada en Valencia se unió formalmente a la Anglican Convocation Europe y se convirtió en la primera congregación de España en alinearse públicamente con el movimiento confesional mundial que rompió con la Iglesia de Inglaterra por la autoridad de las Escrituras.

Abba Post · October 25, 2026Abba Post · 25 de octubre de 2026

Valencia, Spain, now has a visible marker of a schism reshaping Anglicanism worldwide. The Comunidad Anglicana Evangélica de Valencia, a recently formed congregation led by pastor Julian Milson, has formally joined the Anglican Convocation Europe, a network of churches that rejects what it calls the theological drift of the Church of England. Its leaders say the decision was not taken lightly, but that remaining under the historic Spanish Anglican Church, the IERE, had become untenable.

The move mirrors a much larger realignment. Earlier this year, 347 bishops from 27 Anglican provinces signed the Abuja Affirmation in Nigeria, launching a new Global Anglican Communion committed to historic Christian faith and the centrality of the Bible. The signatories argue that the former Anglican Communion, as led by Canterbury, no longer exists in the form it once did, after long disputes over same-sex marriage and the inclusion of LGBT clergy.

For the Valencia congregation, the decisive question was the authority of Scripture. Milson explained that his community wanted to belong to a fellowship where the Bible, rather than cultural pressure, sets the terms of faith and ministry. The Anglican Convocation Europe presents itself not as a breakaway novelty but as the natural heir of historic Anglican doctrine, a claim its critics dispute and its supporters repeat with conviction.

The congregation is small, but its symbolism is large. Spain has long been a country where evangelical Anglicans were a tiny minority within a tiny minority, and a public break of this kind invites both scrutiny and solidarity. Other Spanish congregations watching Valencia will now have a concrete example, in their own language and context, of what joining the confessional movement looks like in practice.

What happens next will be watched well beyond Valencia. If more congregations follow, Spain could become one more front in a global contest over who speaks for Anglicanism. If it stands alone, it will still stand as a testimony: a local church that chose a costly clarity over a comfortable ambiguity, trusting that faithfulness to Scripture is worth the price of separation.

Valencia, España, tiene ahora un marcador visible de un cisma que está reconfigurando el anglicanismo en todo el mundo. La Comunidad Anglicana Evangélica de Valencia, una congregación recién formada y liderada por el pastor Julian Milson, se unió formalmente a la Anglican Convocation Europe, una red de iglesias que rechaza lo que llama la deriva teológica de la Iglesia de Inglaterra. Sus líderes dicen que la decisión no se tomó a la ligera, pero que seguir bajo la histórica Iglesia Española Reformada Episcopal (IERE) se había vuelto insostenible.

El paso refleja una realineación mucho mayor. A principios de este año, 347 obispos de 27 provincias anglicanas firmaron la Afirmación de Abuya en Nigeria y lanzaron una nueva Comunión Anglicana Global comprometida con la fe cristiana histórica y la centralidad de la Biblia. Los firmantes sostienen que la antigua Comunión Anglicana, tal como la lideraba Canterbury, ya no existe en la forma que tuvo, tras largas disputas sobre el matrimonio entre personas del mismo sexo y la inclusión de clero LGBT.

Para la congregación de Valencia, la cuestión decisiva fue la autoridad de las Escrituras. Milson explicó que su comunidad quería pertenecer a una comunión donde la Biblia, y no la presión cultural, establezca los términos de la fe y el ministerio. La Anglican Convocation Europe se presenta no como una novedad separatista, sino como la heredera natural de la doctrina anglicana histórica, una afirmación que sus críticos discuten y sus partidarios repiten con convicción.

La congregación es pequeña, pero su simbolismo es grande. España ha sido durante mucho tiempo un país donde los anglicanos evangélicos eran una diminuta minoría dentro de otra minoría, y una ruptura pública de este tipo atrae tanto escrutinio como solidaridad. Otras congregaciones españolas que observen Valencia tendrán ahora un ejemplo concreto, en su propio idioma y contexto, de cómo se ve en la práctica unirse al movimiento confesional.

Lo que ocurra después se seguirá mucho más allá de Valencia. Si más congregaciones siguen el camino, España podría convertirse en otro frente de una disputa mundial sobre quién habla en nombre del anglicanismo. Si queda sola, seguirá siendo un testimonio: una iglesia local que eligió una claridad costosa antes que una ambigüedad cómoda, confiando en que la fidelidad a las Escrituras vale el precio de la separación.

Reformation Sunday: Churches Mark 509 Years Since Luther's Challenge Shook the ChurchDomingo de la Reforma: las iglesias conmemoran 509 años desde que el desafío de Lutero sacudió a la Iglesia

On Reformation Sunday, October 25, Protestant congregations remembered Martin Luther's 95 Theses of October 31, 1517, and reflected on a Reformation that did not end in the sixteenth century but continues in every generation that returns to Scripture.En el Domingo de la Reforma, el 25 de octubre, las congregaciones protestantes recordaron las 95 tesis de Martín Lutero del 31 de octubre de 1517 y reflexionaron sobre una Reforma que no terminó en el siglo XVI, sino que continúa en cada generación que vuelve a las Escrituras.

Abba Post · October 25, 2026Abba Post · 25 de octubre de 2026

On Sunday, October 25, churches across the Protestant world marked Reformation Sunday, the annual observance that falls on the last Sunday of October. The date points back to October 31, 1517, when the German monk Martin Luther set out his 95 Theses challenging the sale of indulgences and calling the Church back to repentance, grace and the authority of God's Word. Five hundred and nine years later, the anniversary still gathers congregations that trace their spiritual lineage to that protest.

This year's observance carried a familiar tension, and a healthy one. Pastors reminded their flocks that Luther himself was a sinner as well as a reformer, and that celebrating the Reformation is not the same as celebrating Luther the man. The Reformers' own motto, semper reformanda, the church always being reformed according to the Word of God, points away from hero worship and toward continual repentance.

In many congregations the readings told the story plainly. Jeremiah's promise of a new covenant, written on hearts rather than stone tablets, and Jesus' words in John 8 about truth setting people free, framed sermons on grace that cannot be bought, earned or inherited. The message was deliberately personal: reformation is not only something that happened to the Church in Europe, but something God does in individual lives when human effort fails and grace becomes visible.

The observance also looked forward. Churches used the Sunday to renew their stewardship, their teaching and their mission, asking where comfort, tradition or cultural accommodation may have dulled their witness. For younger believers especially, the Reformers' insistence that ordinary Christians can read Scripture for themselves remains a radical invitation, not a museum piece.

As October 31 approaches, the anniversary itself will bring concerts, lectures and special services around the world. But the heart of Reformation Sunday is simpler, and it was heard again this weekend in pulpits large and small: the Church stands, and falls, on the gospel of grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone, according to Scripture alone.

El domingo 25 de octubre, iglesias de todo el mundo protestante celebraron el Domingo de la Reforma, la conmemoración anual que cae el último domingo de octubre. La fecha remite al 31 de octubre de 1517, cuando el monje alemán Martín Lutero expuso sus 95 tesis contra la venta de indulgencias y llamó a la Iglesia a volver al arrepentimiento, la gracia y la autoridad de la Palabra de Dios. Quinientos nueve años después, el aniversario sigue reuniendo a congregaciones que trazan su linaje espiritual hasta aquella protesta.

La conmemoración de este año trajo una tensión familiar, y saludable. Los pastores recordaron a sus congregaciones que el propio Lutero fue pecador además de reformador, y que celebrar la Reforma no es lo mismo que celebrar al hombre. El lema de los reformadores, semper reformanda, la iglesia siempre reformándose conforme a la Palabra de Dios, aparta de la adoración de héroes y apunta a un arrepentimiento continuo.

En muchas congregaciones, las lecturas contaron la historia con claridad. La promesa de Jeremías de un nuevo pacto, escrito en corazones y no en tablas de piedra, y las palabras de Jesús en Juan 8 sobre la verdad que hace libres, enmarcaron sermones sobre una gracia que no se puede comprar, ganar ni heredar. El mensaje fue deliberadamente personal: la reforma no es solo algo que le ocurrió a la Iglesia en Europa, sino algo que Dios hace en vidas individuales cuando el esfuerzo humano fracasa y la gracia se hace visible.

La conmemoración también miró hacia adelante. Las iglesias aprovecharon el domingo para renovar su mayordomía, su enseñanza y su misión, preguntándose dónde la comodidad, la tradición o la adaptación cultural pudieron haber opacado su testimonio. Para los creyentes más jóvenes en especial, la insistencia de los reformadores en que los cristianos comunes pueden leer las Escrituras por sí mismos sigue siendo una invitación radical, no una pieza de museo.

Al acercarse el 31 de octubre, el aniversario mismo traerá conciertos, conferencias y cultos especiales en todo el mundo. Pero el corazón del Domingo de la Reforma es más sencillo, y volvió a escucharse este fin de semana en púlpitos grandes y pequeños: la Iglesia se sostiene, o se cae, sobre el evangelio de la gracia sola, por la fe sola, en Cristo solo, conforme a la Escritura sola.

Afghanistan's First Christian TV Channel Goes on Air Despite Taliban RuleEl primer canal cristiano de televisión de Afganistán sale al aire pese al régimen talibán

Mozhda TV, described by its founders as the first Afghan Christian television channel, began broadcasting on Saturday, October 24, carrying the gospel in Afghan languages to a country where public Christian worship is forbidden.Mozhda TV, descrito por sus fundadores como el primer canal cristiano de televisión afgano, comenzó a emitir el sábado 24 de octubre, llevando el evangelio en lenguas afganas a un país donde el culto cristiano público está prohibido.

Abba Post · October 24, 2026Abba Post · 24 de octubre de 2026

On Saturday, October 24, a small but historic broadcast began. Mozhda TV, which its founders describe as the first Afghan Christian television channel, went on air as church leaders, mission partners and Afghan believers gathered to mark its launch. In a country where the Taliban regime forbids public Christian worship and conversion from Islam can carry a death sentence, the very existence of the channel is an act of faith.

The channel's name carries its message: mozhda means good news. Its programming is produced in the languages Afghan people actually speak at home, aiming to reach viewers inside Afghanistan and the large Afghan diaspora scattered across the world. For isolated believers who cannot safely gather in a church building, a television signal can become, in effect, a congregation that meets in a living room.

The launch comes at a moment of intense pressure on Afghanistan's tiny Christian community. Since the Taliban's return to power, believers have worshipped in secret, in small groups, in whispered prayers. International monitors consistently rank Afghanistan among the most dangerous places on earth to follow Christ. A broadcast ministry cannot replace the gathered church, but it can remind a scattered flock that it is not forgotten.

Those behind the channel are under no illusions about the risks. Broadcasting Christian content into a country ruled by an Islamist regime invites surveillance, blocking and retaliation against anyone found watching. The founders have therefore built the work around discretion and prayer, trusting that God works through means that look fragile to the world.

For the wider Church, Mozhda TV is a call to pray with specificity: for the safety of Afghan viewers, for the presenters and technicians whose voices now carry across borders, and for a day when Afghan Christians can worship openly. Until that day, a television signal crossing a closed border is itself a sermon, that the good news cannot be locked out.

El sábado 24 de octubre comenzó una emisión pequeña pero histórica. Mozhda TV, descrito por sus fundadores como el primer canal cristiano de televisión afgano, salió al aire mientras líderes de iglesias, socios misioneros y creyentes afganos se reunían para celebrar su lanzamiento. En un país donde el régimen talibán prohíbe el culto cristiano público y la conversión desde el islam puede acarrear la pena de muerte, la sola existencia del canal es un acto de fe.

El nombre del canal lleva su mensaje: mozhda significa buenas noticias. Su programación se produce en las lenguas que los afganos hablan realmente en casa, con el fin de llegar a espectadores dentro de Afganistán y a la gran diáspora afgana dispersa por el mundo. Para creyentes aislados que no pueden reunirse con seguridad en un templo, una señal de televisión puede convertirse, en efecto, en una congregación que se reúne en una sala de estar.

El lanzamiento llega en un momento de intensa presión sobre la diminuta comunidad cristiana de Afganistán. Desde el regreso de los talibanes al poder, los creyentes adoran en secreto, en grupos pequeños, en oraciones susurradas. Los observatorios internacionales clasifican sistemáticamente a Afganistán entre los lugares más peligrosos del mundo para seguir a Cristo. Un ministerio televisivo no puede sustituir a la iglesia reunida, pero puede recordar a un rebaño disperso que no ha sido olvidado.

Quienes están detrás del canal no se hacen ilusiones sobre los riesgos. Emitir contenido cristiano hacia un país gobernado por un régimen islamista invita a la vigilancia, el bloqueo y las represalias contra cualquiera que sea descubierto viendo la emisión. Por eso los fundadores han construido el trabajo sobre la discreción y la oración, confiando en que Dios obra por medios que parecen frágiles al mundo.

Para la Iglesia en general, Mozhda TV es un llamado a orar con precisión: por la seguridad de los espectadores afganos, por los presentadores y técnicos cuyas voces cruzan ahora fronteras, y por un día en que los cristianos afganos puedan adorar abiertamente. Hasta ese día, una señal de televisión que cruza una frontera cerrada es en sí misma un sermón: que las buenas noticias no pueden ser encerradas fuera.

Abba Post · October 23, 2026Abba Post · 23 de octubre de 2026

For more than a year, Amin Ismail sat in Kobar Prison in Khartoum, one of Sudan's harshest facilities. He was not there for any crime he had committed. According to International Christian Concern, which reported his release on October 23, Ismail's offence in the eyes of his captors was his Christian faith, and his refusal to abandon it.

Kobar has long been feared in Sudan. Built for political prisoners and hardened criminals, its overcrowding, poor food and arbitrary discipline have made it a byword for suffering. For a Christian convert, detention there also meant isolation from family, from church fellowship and from any public acknowledgement that his case mattered. Months passed with little news, and every week of silence weighed heavier on those praying for him.

Sudan's Christians live inside a war as well as a persecution. Since the civil conflict erupted, the capital has been battered by drone strikes and street fighting, and churches have been damaged, looted or forced to close. In that chaos, individual prisoners can disappear from view entirely, which is why advocacy groups treated Ismail's case as a test of whether anyone was still watching.

His release, then, is more than a personal deliverance. It is evidence that sustained prayer and advocacy still matter, even when a case drags past a year and headlines move on. Families of other detained believers will read this news the way a thirsty traveller reads of water, as proof that freedom, however delayed, is still possible.

The Church now has a responsibility that matches the good news: to pray for Ismail's recovery, for protection in the fragile days after release, and for the many Sudanese Christians still detained, displaced or worshipping under fire. One man has walked out of Kobar. The prayer now is that his freedom becomes a door, not an exception.

Según International Christian Concern, que informó de su liberación el 23 de octubre, el delito de Ismail a ojos de sus captores era su fe cristiana y su negativa a abandonarla.

Para un converso cristiano, la detención allí significaba también aislamiento de la familia, de la comunión de la iglesia y de cualquier reconocimiento público de que su caso importaba. Pasaron meses con pocas noticias, y cada semana de silencio pesaba más sobre quienes oraban por él.

Los cristianos de Sudán viven dentro de una guerra además de una persecución. Desde que estalló el conflicto civil, la capital ha sido sacudida por ataques con drones y combates callejeros, y las iglesias han resultado dañadas, saqueadas o forzadas a cerrar. En ese caos, los presos individuales pueden desaparecer por completo de la vista, por lo que los grupos de defensa trataron el caso de Ismail como una prueba de si alguien seguía vigilando.

Su liberación, por tanto, es más que una liberación personal. Es evidencia de que la oración sostenida y la defensa siguen importando, incluso cuando un caso se prolonga más de un año y los titulares pasan a otra cosa. Las familias de otros creyentes detenidos leerán esta noticia como un viajero sediento lee sobre agua: como prueba de que la libertad, por tardía que sea, sigue siendo posible.

La Iglesia tiene ahora una responsabilidad a la altura de la buena noticia: orar por la recuperación de Ismail, por su protección en los frágiles días posteriores a la liberación, y por los muchos cristianos sudaneses aún detenidos, desplazados o adorando bajo fuego. Un hombre salió de Kobar. La oración ahora es que su libertad se convierta en una puerta, no en una excepción.

On October 9, Nepalese police entered a Bible college in Morang District, in eastern Nepal, and arrested 24 South Korean Christians. The group was accused of conducting illegal religious teaching and training, according to reports summarised in a weekly roundup of Christian news ending October 24. The arrests were carried out in a single operation, and the Christians were taken into custody together.

Nepal's constitution guarantees religious freedom, but it also prohibits proselytising and the conversion of another person, and those provisions have increasingly been used against Christian activity. Christians make up roughly five percent of Nepal's population, and most of them are converts from Hinduism, which makes their churches a visible target whenever nationalist sentiment rises.

The presence of South Korean mission workers adds a diplomatic dimension. South Korean churches have sent missionaries around the world for decades, and Nepal has been one of their fields. When foreign workers are arrested in a group, the case quickly becomes a test of how a host government balances its constitutional promises against political pressure from religious nationalists.

For Nepal's own Christians, the deeper fear is precedent. If a Bible college, a place of teaching rather than street preaching, can be raided and its visitors arrested, then ordinary congregations may reasonably ask what protection they have. Pastors in Nepal already worship with a degree of caution; a high-profile arrest hardens that caution into fear.

The Church worldwide is called to watch this case closely and pray for the detained believers, for fair legal process, and for Nepal's Christians, both Nepali and foreign, who chose a costly obedience in a country where following Jesus is legal in name and dangerous in practice. Their arrest is a reminder that religious freedom is not secured by a paragraph in a constitution, but by the daily courage of governments to honour it.

Para los propios cristianos de Nepal, el temor más profundo es el precedente. Los pastores de Nepal ya adoran con cierto grado de cautela; una detención de alto perfil endurece esa cautela hasta convertirla en temor.

La Iglesia en todo el mundo está llamada a seguir este caso de cerca y orar por los creyentes detenidos, por un proceso legal justo y por los cristianos de Nepal, tanto nepalíes como extranjeros, que eligieron una obediencia costosa en un país donde seguir a Jesús es legal en el nombre y peligroso en la práctica. In northeastern Nigeria, a Christian mother's conversation has cost her fourteen years of freedom. International Christian Concern reported on October 21 that the woman was sentenced to 14 years in prison after sharing her faith with a Muslim woman, who later testified that she had subsequently converted to Christianity. The sentence turns an ordinary act of witness into a criminal offence.

The case sits inside Nigeria's wider crisis for Christians. In the north-east, sharia-based legal systems operate alongside federal law, and blasphemy and conversion-related prosecutions have repeatedly been used against Christians. For a mother, the sentence is doubly cruel: it removes her from her children and punishes the very act of telling another person about Jesus, which Christians understand as obedience, not provocation.

Nigeria remains the deadliest country in the world to be a Christian. The 2026 World Watch List and independent monitors agree that thousands of Christians have been killed there in recent years, most of them in the Middle Belt and the north. Against that background of lethal violence, a prison sentence for evangelism can look almost bureaucratic, but for the woman and her family it is no less devastating.

Her case also exposes a painful asymmetry. The woman who converted testified about her own faith decision, yet it is the Christian who shared the gospel who bears the punishment. Advocates say such prosecutions are designed to chill witness itself: if telling a neighbour about Christ can bring fourteen years in prison, many believers will understandably fall silent.

The global Church should refuse that silence on her behalf. Prayer for her endurance, for her children, and for a legal outcome that restores her freedom is the immediate calling. So is advocacy: that Nigeria's federal authorities honour the religious freedom their own constitution promises, and that no mother anywhere is imprisoned for speaking the name of Jesus to a friend.

The bill is currently undergoing pre-legislative scrutiny by a joint committee of MPs and peers, which will report to the government by January 2027.

The groups' central complaint is about what the bill does not say. There is nothing on the face of the Bill, they wrote, which reassures Bible-believing Christians that religious teaching, pastoral care, prayer, or the ordinary work of churches could not be subject to prosecution. Courts will apply the statutory words, not a newspaper article, they added, answering the minister's public claim that the proposals do not threaten religious freedom.

They also objected to the bill's language itself. The deliberate use of the word conversion, a foundational Christian term describing the work of the Holy Spirit in turning a person to Christ, to label the conduct to be criminalised is particularly ill-judged, they wrote, calling on the minister to abandon the terminology and adopt language that does not appropriate or distort a core Christian concept.

The Evangelical Alliance has said it will work with others to challenge the proposals, and Christian Concern has warned that the bill opens the door to false accusations against parents and pastors. For evangelicals across Britain, the coming months of committee scrutiny will be decisive: either the bill gains explicit protections for prayer, preaching and pastoral care, or the ordinary work of churches will be carried out under the shadow of a criminal law.

Gold Treasure Survives 1,400 Years at an Ancient Christian Monastery in the Judean DesertUn tesoro de oro sobrevive 1.400 años en un antiguo monasterio cristiano del desierto de Judea

Israeli archaeologists have unearthed a rare 1,400-year-old gold treasure, including Byzantine coins and an ornate ring, at the Hyrcania monastery site in the Judean Desert, shedding new light on the region's early Christian monastic life.Arqueólogos israelíes desenterraron un raro tesoro de oro de 1.400 años, con monedas bizantinas y un anillo ornamentado, en el sitio del monasterio de Hircania en el desierto de Judea, arrojando nueva luz sobre la vida monástica cristiana primitiva de la región.

Abba Post · October 25, 2026Abba Post · 25 de octubre de 2026

Israeli archaeologists have announced a discovery that survived fourteen centuries of looting, weather and war. At the ancient site of Hyrcania in the northern Judean Desert, about ten miles east of Jerusalem, researchers unearthed a rare gold treasure: two gold coins and a delicate gold ring, hidden and preserved for 1,400 years at a former Christian monastery.

The coins are solidi bearing the image of Heraclius, who ruled the Byzantine Empire from 610 to 641 AD. The site itself is older still: founded in the late Hellenistic period, between the second and first centuries BC, as a fortress, it later became a Christian monastery associated with the activity of Saint Sabbas, one of the founders of desert monasticism, and operated for hundreds of years.

The announcement was made this month by Israel's Coordinator of Government Activities in the Territories. Its Archaeology Unit, which safeguards heritage in the area, said the finds attest to Christian activity in the region during the Byzantine era. Archaeologist Binyamin Har-Even said the artefacts reflect an important chapter from the Byzantine period and the early Christian tradition in the region.

What makes the survival remarkable is the site's vulnerability. Hyrcania has long been exposed to antiquities looting, and gold, above all, tends to disappear first. That these coins and this ring remained, in a place monks once chose for prayer precisely because it was remote and harsh, feels like a small providence of preservation.

These discoveries reinforce the understanding that Hyrcania was part of the monastic and religious landscape active in the Judean Desert during the Byzantine period, the archaeology unit said. For believers today, the treasure is more than museum material: it is physical evidence that the desert fathers and mothers who sought God in silence left traces that still speak, fourteen centuries later, of a faith that endured in the wilderness.

Arqueólogos israelíes anunciaron un descubrimiento que sobrevivió a catorce siglos de saqueos, clima y guerra. En el antiguo sitio de Hircania, en el norte del desierto de Judea, a unas diez millas al este de Jerusalén, los investigadores desenterraron un raro tesoro de oro: dos monedas de oro y un delicado anillo de oro, escondidos y conservados durante 1.400 años en un antiguo monasterio cristiano.

Las monedas son sólidos con la imagen de Heraclio, quien gobernó el Imperio Bizantino entre 610 y 641 d. C. El sitio en sí es aún más antiguo: fundado a finales del período helenístico, entre los siglos II y I a. C., como fortaleza, más tarde se convirtió en un monasterio cristiano asociado a la actividad de San Sabas, uno de los fundadores del monasticismo del desierto, y funcionó durante cientos de años.

El anuncio lo hizo este mes el Coordinador de Actividades Gubernamentales en los Territorios de Israel. Su Unidad de Arqueología, que protege el patrimonio de la zona, dijo que los hallazgos atestiguan la actividad cristiana en la región durante la era bizantina. El arqueólogo Binyamin Har-Even dijo que los objetos reflejan un capítulo importante del período bizantino y de la tradición cristiana primitiva en la región.

Lo que hace notable la supervivencia es la vulnerabilidad del sitio. Hircania ha estado expuesta durante mucho tiempo al saqueo de antigüedades, y el oro, sobre todo, tiende a desaparecer primero. Que estas monedas y este anillo hayan permanecido, en un lugar que los monjes eligieron para la oración precisamente porque era remoto y duro, se siente como una pequeña providencia de preservación.

Estos descubrimientos refuerzan la comprensión de que Hircania formaba parte del paisaje monástico y religioso activo en el desierto de Judea durante el período bizantino, dijo la unidad de arqueología. Para los creyentes de hoy, el tesoro es más que material de museo: es evidencia física de que los padres y madres del desierto que buscaron a Dios en silencio dejaron huellas que aún hablan, catorce siglos después, de una fe que perduró en el desierto.

Mission Cry Sends Half a Million Dollars in Bibles and Books to Hong Kong While the Door Is Still OpenMission Cry envía medio millón de dólares en Biblias y libros a Hong Kong mientras la puerta sigue abierta

Abba Post · October 23, 2026Abba Post · 23 de octubre de 2026

As China continues to exert its influence over its neighbours, a mission is taking the remaining opportunity to distribute Scripture. Mission Cry, a ministry that sends free Bibles and Christian books around the world, is concerned about China's influence over Hong Kong, Japan and Taiwan, and worried that it will soon affect the ability to import Christian materials. Its response is not retreat, but urgency.

Jason Woolford, speaking with Mission Network News, said the ministry plans to send about half a million dollars' worth of free Bibles and Christian books to Hong Kong, reaching well over 30,000 people. The materials cover a wide range of reading levels and ministry needs, from Sunday school materials and Bibles to seminary literature, engaging the unsaved, new believers, mature Christians and ministers in training.

The maths of the mission is striking. Woolford says the ministry's experience is that every book it sends is read by around 20 people. If that pattern holds, a shipment valued at half a million dollars could pass through the hands of hundreds of thousands of readers, in homes, churches and classrooms that may one day find such materials much harder to obtain.

Hong Kong's position makes the timing significant. The city has long been a gateway for Christian literature into the wider region, and its churches have enjoyed freedoms that are increasingly fragile. A window that is open today may narrow tomorrow, and Mission Cry is acting as though the window matters more than the risk.

For supporters and praying churches, the story is a reminder that mission is often a race against closing doors. Bibles are not only for the moment they are received; they are seeds for a future in which the reader may need them most. Sending them while they can still be sent is, in itself, an act of faith in a God who opens doors, and in a Church willing to walk through them before they shut.

Los materiales cubren una amplia gama de niveles de lectura y necesidades ministeriales, desde materiales de escuela dominical y Biblias hasta literatura de seminario, y se dirigen a no creyentes, nuevos creyentes, cristianos maduros y ministros en formación.

Las cuentas de la misión son llamativas. Woolford dice que la experiencia del ministerio es que cada libro que envía es leído por unas 20 personas. Para los donantes y las iglesias que oran, la historia recuerda que la misión es a menudo una carrera contra puertas que se cierran. Enviarlas mientras aún se pueden enviar es, en sí mismo, un acto de fe en un Dios que abre puertas, y en una Iglesia dispuesta a cruzarlas antes de que se cierren.

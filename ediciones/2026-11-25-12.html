T. Russell Hunter, founder of Abolitionists Rising in Oklahoma, was arrested on Wednesday, November 18, at the University of Texas at Dallas in Richardson on a charge of criminal trespass. Hunter had spent about two and a half hours talking with students about abortion and Christianity in the campus's central plaza, known as the Plinth, when associate dean Tricia Losavio called police because he was not affiliated with a student group.

According to his group and his attorney, C. J. Grisham, police handcuffed him within about 90 seconds to two minutes. He spent roughly 24 hours in custody and was released on bond on Thursday, November 19. Hunter and Grisham say they will challenge the charge and sue the university and its police, arguing that the arrest was a "heckler's veto" against free speech.

The university said the group had no invitation from any student organization and cited Texas law SB 2972. A police log confirms a trespass incident at 1:46 p.m. on November 18 (case 202600014595). The video of the arrest circulated widely this week, drawing attention from free-speech advocates across the political spectrum.

The case turns on a simple question: does a public university's plaza belong to the public, or only to the public the university invites? A campus that welcomes debate in theory but arrests the debater in practice has not resolved that question — it has only shown which side of it the institution stands on.

For Christians, the stakes are broader than one arrest. If speaking about abortion and the Gospel on a public campus can end in handcuffs, then the free exchange of ideas that universities exist to protect is already in trouble. Hunter's lawsuit will test whether the First Amendment still means what it says — in Texas, and everywhere else.
